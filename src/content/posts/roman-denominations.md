Roman money changed more than most people expect. The denarius that paid a legionary under Augustus was not the coin that paid one under Gallienus, though both carried the name. Weights fell, silver was diluted, and denominations were invented, abandoned and revived over roughly seven hundred years. What follows is the sequence, with numbers where the numbers are known.

## Weighed, not counted

The earliest Roman money was not coinage. *Aes rude* — irregular lumps of bronze — circulated by weight from perhaps the eighth century BC. It was followed by *aes signatum*, cast bronze bars stamped with designs, produced from around 280 BC and weighing on the order of 1.5 kg apiece. Neither was struck, and neither had a face value. You put them on a scale.

Cast round coinage, *aes grave*, appears around the same period. Its unit was the **as**, at first tied to the Roman pound, the *libra* of roughly 324 g. A full-weight libral as is a formidable object, closer to a discus than a coin. Its fractions were marked with pellets: the semis at one half (S), the triens at one third, the quadrans at a quarter, the sextans at a sixth, and the uncia at one twelfth.

That standard did not hold. The as was reduced repeatedly through the third and second centuries BC — to semi-libral weight during the Hannibalic war, then sextantal, then uncial around 141 BC, and finally semiuncial under the Lex Papiria, conventionally dated to about 91 BC. By the end, an as weighed a fraction of what its name originally implied.

## The denarius

The **denarius** was introduced during the Second Punic War, around 211 BC. That date rests on hoard evidence assembled in the twentieth century; Pliny reports a much earlier date, and the question was genuinely open until the excavation of the Morgantina hoard settled it in favour of the later figure.

The new coin weighed about 4.5 g — one seventy-second of a pound — and was struck in nearly pure silver. Its name records its value: *deni*, "containing ten", because it was tariffed at ten bronze asses, and marked accordingly with an X. Alongside it came the **quinarius** at five asses and the **sestertius** at two and a half, marked IIS, from which the later abbreviation HS derives. A separate coin, the **victoriatus** at roughly 3.4 g and lower fineness, seems to have been struck for circulation outside Rome.

Around 141 BC the denarius was retariffed from ten asses to sixteen. Why is not settled — the usual explanation is that the bronze had been reduced so far that the old ratio no longer reflected metal values, but the sources are thin. The denarius mark changes to XVI, or to a crossed X, and the weight settles at about 3.9 g, one eighty-fourth of a pound.

Gold was struck only sporadically under the Republic. It becomes a regular part of the system with Caesar, whose **aureus** was cut at one fortieth of a pound, about 8.1 g.

## Augustus settles the system

The Augustan reform, usually placed around 23 BC, produced the arrangement that held for the next two and a half centuries:

- **Aureus**, gold, about 7.85 g — worth 25 denarii
- **Denarius**, silver, about 3.9 g — worth 4 sestertii or 16 asses
- **Sestertius**, brass, about 25–28 g
- **Dupondius**, brass, about 12.5 g — 2 asses
- **As**, copper, about 11 g
- **Semis**, brass, about 3.5 g — half an as
- **Quadrans**, copper, about 3 g — a quarter of an as

The metal mattered as much as the size. The sestertius and dupondius were struck in *orichalcum*, a brass alloy that is distinctly yellower than the red copper of the as. Since a dupondius and an as were close in diameter, colour was how you told two asses from one. From Nero onward the dupondius also carried a radiate crown, which made the distinction visible even on a worn coin.

The same logic runs to the bottom of the system. A semis and a quadrans weigh about the same, but the semis is brass and passes for twice the copper quadrans. Metal, not mass, set the value.

Almost every imperial bronze of this period carries the letters **SC**, for *senatus consulto*, by decree of the senate. The traditional reading is that Augustus left base metal to the senate and kept gold and silver for himself, a split that mattered because armies were paid in precious metal. How much the letters really signify is disputed. They may mark authority over striking, or only the authorisation of the types and honours shown.

## What actually circulated

A system on paper is not the same as the coins in a purse. A dolium set into the counter of the thermopolium of Vetutius Placidus at Pompeii held 1,385 bronze coins, some 585 sestertii in all — likely several days' takings rather than one afternoon's. Asses account for 63.3% of them. The small change the system implies should be everywhere is almost absent: one semis, one triens, four quadrantes, one sextans.

For scale, a labourer in the early empire could expect around a denarius a day. On that reckoning an aureus was close to a month's wages, and the as that bought lunch at a Pompeian counter was a fraction of a morning's.

## Nero's reduction

In AD 64 Nero cut both precious-metal denominations. The aureus fell from one forty-second of a pound to one forty-fifth, about 7.3 g. The denarius fell from one eighty-fourth to one ninety-sixth, about 3.4 g, and its fineness dropped from roughly 98% to around 93%.

This is the first imperial debasement of consequence, and it set the pattern. Fineness declined through the second century — to around 89% under Trajan, around 79% under Marcus Aurelius — then fell off a cliff under Septimius Severus, reaching roughly 50% by the end of his reign.

The [denarii in my cabinet](/hobbies/coins/collection) trace part of this. A Nerva of AD 96–98 weighs 3.75 g; a Trajan 3.29 g; a Hadrian 3.23 g; a Caracalla of AD 212 only 2.49 g. Individual coins vary, and one specimen proves nothing on its own — Roman mints worked *al marco*, striking a fixed number of coins from a fixed weight of metal rather than adjusting each blank. But the direction is not in doubt.

## The antoninianus

In AD 215 Caracalla introduced a new silver coin, distinguished by the radiate crown the emperor wears on it. Its ancient name is unknown; numismatists call it the **antoninianus**, after Caracalla's official name, Antoninus.

It was tariffed at two denarii but weighed only about 5.1 g against the denarius's 3.4 g — roughly one and a half times the metal for twice the face value. That gap is the whole story of the third century in miniature. The antoninianus drove out the denarius, which had effectively ceased to be struck by the middle of the century.

Then it debased in turn. Fineness fell from around 50% under Caracalla to about 40% under Gordian III, to somewhere near 15–20% under Valerian and Gallienus, and by around AD 270 to a few per cent — a copper coin given a thin silver wash that wore off in circulation. Weight drifted down with it.

Here the cabinet is unusually legible. A Gordian III antoninianus of AD 241–243 weighs 4.05 g and a Philip I of AD 244–247 weighs 4.29 g, both still recognisably silver. The Valerian of AD 253–260, at 3.96 g, is catalogued as billon — the word for silver so diluted with copper that it no longer looks like silver at all.

## Aurelian starts to repair it

Aurelian reformed the coinage in AD 274. The new radiate is heavier and better made, around 3.9 g with something like 5% silver, and many examples carry the mark **XXI** — KA in the Greek-speaking east.

What XXI means is disputed. The common reading is a ratio: twenty parts copper to one of silver, an assay statement. Others read it as a value in sestertii. The evidence does not settle it, and anyone who tells you otherwise is choosing a side. Aurelian also restored the aureus to about one fiftieth of a pound.

## Diocletian starts again

Diocletian's reform of AD 294 rebuilt the system from the top:

- **Aureus** at one sixtieth of a pound, about 5.45 g
- **Argenteus**, good silver at about 3.4 g — a deliberate echo of the Neronian denarius
- A large silver-washed bronze of about 10 g, with a radiate fraction and a laureate fraction below it

The ancient name of that large bronze is not securely known. Modern catalogues call it a **follis** or a **nummus**; both are conventions, and you will see the same coin filed under either. My own Constantinian pieces are catalogued both ways for exactly this reason.

Whether the reform worked is another open question. It was followed in AD 301 by the Currency Edict and the Edict on Maximum Prices, which fixed prices across the empire and, by the testimony of Lactantius, failed. Historians disagree about whether Diocletian was trying to halt inflation, to reprice the coinage against it, or to fund the army and the tetrarchic building programme.

## The solidus

Constantine introduced the **solidus** around AD 309–310, probably at Trier, though AD 312 is also argued. It was struck at one seventy-second of a Roman pound — about 4.5 g of gold — and unlike almost everything that preceded it, that standard held. The solidus remained the anchor of Mediterranean gold for the better part of seven hundred years, passing into Byzantine coinage as the *nomisma*. Silver returned as the **siliqua** and the heavier **miliarense**.

The base metal kept shrinking. The nummus went from roughly 10 g at Diocletian's reform to a fraction of that within a few decades, and the fourth-century bronze denominations changed so often, under names we mostly do not know, that catalogues fall back on the modern labels AE1 to AE4, which describe only diameter.

That shrinkage is visible in four coins struck within a decade of each other: a follis of AD 315–316 at 4.11 g, one of AD 318 at 3.78 g, a nummus of AD 318–319 at 2.11 g, and one of AD 321–324 at 2.96 g. Not a clean line — mint, officina and issue all matter, and these come from Lugdunum, Siscia, Ticinum and Nicomedia rather than one series — but the coin the state handed out was getting smaller.

## The system at a glance

| Denomination | Metal | Approx. weight | Period | Value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| As (libral) | Bronze, cast | ~324 g | from c. 280 BC | Unit |
| Denarius | Silver | 4.5 g → 3.9 g | from c. 211 BC | 10, later 16 asses |
| Sestertius | Silver, then brass | 1.1 g → ~27 g | from c. 211 BC | ¼ denarius |
| Aureus | Gold | ~8.1 → 7.3 g | Caesar onward | 25 denarii |
| Dupondius | Brass | ~12.5 g | Augustan | 2 asses |
| Antoninianus | Silver → billon | 5.1 g → ~3 g | AD 215–294 | 2 denarii |
| Argenteus | Silver | ~3.4 g | from AD 294 | — |
| Follis / nummus | Silvered bronze | ~10 g, falling | from AD 294 | — |
| Solidus | Gold | ~4.5 g | from c. AD 310 | 1/72 pound |
| Siliqua | Silver | ~3.4 → 2.2 g | 4th century | — |

Two threads run through all of it. Precious-metal coins lost weight and fineness whenever the state needed more of them than the mines produced. And the names outlived the things: a third-century denarius shared almost nothing with Caesar's except a word, which is worth remembering whenever an ancient source quotes a price.
